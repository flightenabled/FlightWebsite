Latest motors & props

What the industry is actually certifying

We watch the motors and props the industry is actually certifying — not brochure peaks. Sourced September 2026. This is not Flight Enabled hardware in the air.

  1. 1

    Published aviation motors that are certified or near it sit roughly 4–6 kW/kg the way the manufacturer counted. Safran’s ENGINeUS 100 (125 kW, about 5 kW/kg, air-cooled) is the one EASA has actually certified for new air mobility.

  2. 2

    BETA publishes an H500B pusher at 640 kW max / 300 kW continuous, 75 kg with inverters (6.0 / 4.2 kW/kg), and a V600A lift motor at 1,500 rpm (4.2 kW/kg continuous). magniX sits in the same 165–515 kW continuous class on test airframes — not on a bench in Murray today.

  3. 3

    Hartzell and BETA got the first U.S. Part 35 propeller type certificate written around an electric engine (21 Jul 2025): 5-blade carbon, ground-adjustable fixed pitch. Diameter is not in the cert announcements, so we don’t invent one.

  4. 4

    No U.S. passenger eVTOL type certificate as of 2 Sep 2026. Joby and Archer have FAA 21.17(b) special-class criteria, not a TC. EHang’s CAAC paperwork is not an FAA certificate. Passenger layouts are still tilt-six or lift-plus-pusher; quietness is being measured, not solved.

  5. 5

    Industrial heavy-lift still looks like fat outrunners and 36–40 inch carbon props — T-MOTOR U15-class. That is the family of hardware we know from the shop floor, in storage while we look for workspace. It is not a Part 33 engine.

  6. 6

    Flight Enabled is a Utah shop restarting. Hardware in storage, including the skeleton. No flying fleet, no aircraft sales, no type certificate. When benches are up, 4–6 kW/kg certified-class machines and electric-engine props are the class we study — heat, noise, and certification, not a brochure.

Still hard

Peak is not continuous. Dry mass is not installed mass. High voltage at altitude. Community noise. FAA 21.17(b) and EASA SC-VTOL are not one standard. Batteries still limit range. Catalog density claims at 20,000 rpm are not drop-in replacements for a large direct-drive prop.

The aim

Where this is going

  1. 1

    Improve small UAVs iteratively until they can be trusted as safe, quiet, capable package delivery vehicles.

  2. 2

    Then increase size so they can carry passengers and cargo and be piloted as simply as cars.

Autonomous cars are a good hint at the end goal of Flight Enabled: making travel less of a pain, so that people and things are easier to bring together. We are not building robotaxis. Self-driving cars show where this is going; flight is the same idea in the air, so people and cargo can meet without the trip being the hard part.

Enabling flight for everyone should involve everyone.
